### **Introduction to Contracts**

### **Key Takeaways**

   
**Contracts**  
Contract law is a set of rules used to determine whether a promise is enforceable in court

* Contracts for the sale of goods ➔ UCC Article 2  
* All other contracts ➔ Common law

**Enforceable Promises**

* Offer  
  * Communication indicating that the person who made the offer wants to make a deal and be bound by it  
* Acceptance  
  * Communication or an act by the offeree indicating they want to take the deal and be bound by it  
* Consideration  
  * A bargained-for exchange of legal value

**Unilateral Contract**  
Created when the offer requests performance rather than a promise

**Bilateral Contract**  
Created when the offer can be accepted by a promise  
 

### **How Are Offers Made?**

### **Key Takeaways**

   
**Offer:** Communication of willingness to enter into a contract with the offeree

* Must be directed toward a certain person or class of people  
* Must have definite and certain terms so a court is able to figure out a remedy in the event one of the parties breaches the contract  
  * Identity of the offeree, the subject matter, and the price are typically important terms  
  * Certain types of contracts also require certain terms to be provided  
    * Example: Land sale contracts must identify the land and the price terms  
  * The less specific the communication, the less likely a court will find the intent to make an offer

**Common Exam Issue: Is an Advertisement an Offer?**

* Ads are usually not offers, but merely invitations seeking offers  
* If an ad limits the people who can accept, the ad can constitute an offer

A retailer of electronic goods maintains a website. Customers can access the website, see pictures of items the retailer has in inventory, read about their features, and put them in a virtual shopping cart to purchase them.

Would a court likely find that the descriptions of the goods on the website constitute offers?

Correct

No, the descriptions are likely to be seen as invitations seeking offers. Customers placing orders will make offers to the retailer, which can then choose whether to accept them (although, the retailer will almost certainly automatically accept the offer).

A shopper goes into a drugstore, selects an eyeliner pencil, takes it to the cash register, and hands it to the cashier to pay for it.

Did the shopper make an offer? 

Incorrect

Yes, this is the classic retail situation.

The shopper is making an offer to buy the eyeliner, even though the communication here did not involve the use of any words. When the shopper handed the eyeliner to the cashier, the shopper was indicating a desire to buy the eyeliner. That’s the implication whenever you throw something up on the grocery store conveyor belt.

Do we know the terms? What’s the key term in a contract for the sale of goods? The type of goods involved (here, eyeliner) and the quantity (one). We don’t need the price term in an offer, but both parties will presume that the price is whatever the eyeliner was marked at on the shelf.  

A sports shoe manufacturer advertises a payment of $50,000 to the first person who can run from the Liberty Bell to the Vietnam Memorial in a pair of the manufacturer’s shoes.

Did the manufacturer make an offer? 

Correct

Yes, although the communication here is an advertisement, it limits the people who can accept the offer to a single person—the first person who runs from the Liberty Bell to the Vietnam Memorial in a pair of the manufacturer’s shoes.

This is an offer to form a unilateral contract because the manufacturer is seeking performance and not a promise. 

### **Termination of Offers**

### **Key Takeaways**

   
**Passage of Time (Lapse)**

* Offer is open for a particular time and terminates when that time passes  
* If offer doesn’t state a time, it’s open for a reasonable time

**Offeror’s Revocation**

* Offeror may terminate their offer by communicating a revocation of the offer to the offeree before the offeree accepts  
  * Direct revocation ➔ Revocation is directly communicated to offeree  
  * Indirect revocation ➔ Offeree learns from a reliable source that the offeror no longer wishes to make an offer  
* Option Contract: Contract ancillary to an offer promising to keep the offer open  
  * If offeree gives consideration, there is an option contract, and the offer is irrevocable for the time stated  
  * If no consideration, there is no option contract, and the offer is revocable  
* Merchant’s Firm Offer  
  * If merchant promises to keep an offer to sell goods open in a signed writing, the offer will stay open for the stated time, or if no time is stated, for a reasonable time, not to exceed 3 months

**Offeree’s Rejection**

* If offeree rejects the offer, it terminates  
* An offeree’s rejection followed by a later attempted acceptance is treated as counteroffer, which offeror can accept or reject  
* If offeree offers probing questions instead of counteroffering, it is a mere inquiry, which doesn’t terminate the original offer

**Operation of Law**

* If certain circumstances occur **after** the offer is made, the offer will be terminated by operation of law  
  * Offeror or offeree dies  
  * Offeror or offeree becomes legally incompetent  
  * Subject matter of the contract is destroyed  
  * Law change makes the subject matter of the offer illegal

### **Acceptance—Who and How**

### **Key Takeaways**

   
**Acceptance**  
Offeree agreeing to be bound by the offer (assent)

**Who Can Assent?**

* Only party to whom offer is made can accept the offer  
* Offeree must know of the offer to accept

**How Are Offers Accepted?**    
Generally, offers may be accepted in any manner reasonable under the circumstances, typically in the same manner they are made

* Exceptions

  * Offers seeking performance can be accepted only by performance (unilateral contract)  
  * Article 2 ➔ In a contract for the sale of goods, an offer to buy goods for current and prompt shipment can be accepted by either a promise to ship or by current or prompt shipment  
* Silence as acceptance

  * Generally not allowed, unless  
    * Offeror and offeree have had a course of dealing in which silence has been treated as acceptance  
    * If the offeree knowingly and silently takes the benefits of an offeror’s performance, courts will often find acceptance

A cattle dealer negotiated with a breeder for the purchase of a prize bull.

Following the negotiations, the dealer wrote to the breeder offering to buy the bull. He said in the letter, "If I hear nothing further from you, I consider the bull mine for $5,000."

The breeder did not reply to the letter. The bull was subsequently sold in an auction to a third party.   
   
Was there a binding contract between the cattle dealer and the breeder? 

Correct

For an offer to be validly accepted, the offeree must communicate their acceptance to the offeror. Silence cannot constitute acceptance, as a positive act is required. Therefore, (B) is the correct answer. (This rule does not apply if the parties have agreed to dispense with the need for communication, but in this case the breeder has not agreed to this.)

(A) is incorrect because there is no suggestion that the parties did not intend to be legally bound. The parties are acting in the course of business so there is a strong presumption of legal intent and there is no evidence in the scenario to rebut this.

(C) is incorrect because, as stated above, silence cannot constitute acceptance.

(D) is incorrect because the elements for a valid contract are not present, as there has been no valid acceptance by the breeder.

A paper manufacturer offers to sell 100 reams of paper suitable for printing brochures to a printing business at $4 per ream. The offer is on the paper manufacturer’s standard terms and conditions and states, “You are required to e-mail acceptance to your sales associate by 5 p.m.” The terms and conditions go on to state that delivery will be two weeks later, and that payment is required on delivery.

Correct

If an offer prescribes a method of acceptance, that method must be used. In this case, leaving a message on the company’s main phone number when there is no guarantee that the offeror will listen to it, is not the same as directly e-mailing a sales associate, which was the requested method.

(B) is incorrect because, while acceptance by telephone generally can be a valid method of communication, in this particular scenario the requirement was for an e-mail acceptance to the sales associate.

(C) is incorrect because the problem with the valid purported acceptance was not that the message could have been heard by a third party, it is that acceptance by e-mail was prescribed, and therefore had to be used for a valid acceptance. ### **Acceptance—Say What?\!**

### **Key Takeaways**

   
**Mirror Image Rule**  
At common law, the acceptance has to indicate that the offeree accepts each and every term of the offer, otherwise it’s treated as a rejection and counteroffer

**Article 2 Sale of Goods Acceptance**

* An acceptance that contains new or different terms can be effective as an acceptance unless it’s expressly made conditional on acceptance of the new or different terms  
* If both parties are merchants, a new term usually will become part of the contract

**Mailbox Rule**

* An acceptance is effective the moment the acceptance is put in the mailbox properly addressed  
* Doesn’t matter if the acceptance isn’t received, as long as it’s properly addressed  
* Not applicable to rejections or revocations (these are effective when received)  
* Offeror may opt out of the mailbox rule (“Acceptance will be effective only upon receipt")

Incorrect

The correct answer is (D). This question involves an offer (the proposal) to perform a service, which is governed by the common law and not the UCC. Under the common law, to be effective, an acceptance must mirror the terms of the offer. Here the acceptance included a new term (the 45-day completion requirement) and thus was a counteroffer.

(A) is incorrect. It relates to a rule that applies with respect to the sale of goods, and we have a proposal for services here. In a contract for the sale of goods, if both parties are merchants, an acceptance with an additional term can be effective, and the acceptance terms usually govern.

(B), like (A), is incorrect because it relates to a rule that applies with respect to a sale of goods. In such sales, if either party is not a merchant, an acceptance with an additional term is effective, and the terms of the offer usually govern.

(C) is incorrect because it, too, relates to a UCC rule applicable to the sale of goods. Moreover, the acceptance here does not indicate it would be effective only if the proposed term was accepted, so the choice would be wrong even if the question involved the sale of goods.  

Wanting to use her new law office stationery, Amber writes a letter to her friend David on January 16, offering to sell him her bike for $150.

On January 18, David receives the letter.

On January 19, after joining her neighborhood bike club and realizing that she doesn’t have another bike, Amber sends David a letter revoking her offer.

Later that same day, thinking it would be novel to actually write a letter, too, David sends a letter back to Amber saying he’ll take the bike. And since they’re going old school, he includes a $150 check in the letter to pay for the bike.

David receives Amber’s second letter on January 20, and she receives his letter on January 21\.

Was David’s acceptance effective?

This question illustrates both how the mailbox rule works and how easy it is to make mailbox rule questions confusing.

To answer, you must remember the rule, which is pretty simple:   
Acceptances are effective upon mailing and everything else is effective upon receipt.

Knowing that, you filter out all the other dates.

* When was the acceptance mailed? January 19\.  
* When was the revocation received? January 20\.

Since the acceptance was dropped in the mailbox before the revocation was received, the acceptance was effective, and a contract was formed on January 19\.

### **Consideration** 

### **Key Takeaways**

   
**Consideration**  
Bargained-for exchange of legal value

* Contracts valid only if there is a bargain in which something of legal value is exchanged for the promise  
  * Legal Value \= Benefit to promisor or detriment to promisee  
  * Must exist on both sides of the agreement  
* Gifts  
  * An offer to make a gift generally isn’t enforceable, even if it includes a promise, because there is no consideration  
* Consideration doesn’t have to have monetary value  
* Benefit or detriment may include forbearance  
* A promise to do something you’re already legally obligated to do generally isn’t consideration  
* Past consideration (something already given or performed before the promise was made) isn’t consideration

**Exception: Promissory Estoppel/Detrimental Reliance**

* If someone makes a promise, and  
* No consideration is given in return, but  
* The promisor knows that the promisee is likely to rely on the promise to their detriment, and  
* The promisee does in fact rely on the promise, then  
* The promisee’s reliance will substitute for consideration
